Photo picking vs photo culling.

Two names for the same decision, arriving from opposite directions. Culling is the photographer's word: subtract the failures. Picking is the consumer's: surface the winners. Knowing the difference tells you what tool you actually need.

Culling: the subtractive tradition

Culling comes from professional workflow: shoot 500 frames, deliver 40. It's subtractive — the job is finding and removing the failures (soft focus, closed eyes, redundant takes) until what remains is the deliverable. Classically it happens on a desktop, in a rating tool, reviewing chronologically like a contact sheet.

Picking: the additive question

Picking starts from the other end: out of everything here, which ones are worth keeping? It's the consumer's problem — not a shoot to deliver but a camera roll to live with — and it's fundamentally a ranking problem. You don't need every failure removed; you need the winners surfaced.

Where they converge

In practice the two are one loop, and SpectraSort runs it as one loop:

  1. Pick first: the slice comes back ranked — by quality, by your learned taste, or by what you asked for. That's the additive half done before you start.
  2. Cull second: swipe through the ranked review — right to pick, down to keep, left to let go — with duplicates, bursts and look-alikes stacked so each repeated moment costs one decision.
  3. Finish: Batch Process files the picks to an album or the share sheet and clears the rest, through the system dialog.

FAQ

Picking & culling.

Is culling just deleting photos?
No — culling is deciding. Deletion is the optional last step. A cull can end in an album of keepers with nothing deleted at all.
Do I need a culling tool if I'm not a photographer?
The camera roll is the largest unculled photo archive most people own. If your library has thousands of photos and repeated moments, you have a photographer's problem with none of the photographer's tooling — that's exactly what a picker/culler is for.
What does AI actually do in culling?
Three things: it ranks frames before you look (so review starts at the top of the pile), it stacks near-identicals (so a burst costs one decision), and — in SpectraSort's case — it learns your taste from your keeps and rejects, so the ranking gets personal over time.
Can one app do both?
